# 31.1 — Law of One Price

> The bedrock principle under everything: in an efficient market, identical
> assets must trade at the same price, because any gap invites riskless
> arbitrage that closes it. This is the **no-arbitrage** assumption that powers
> derivative pricing — it is what makes put-call parity binding — and it shows up
> again as Purchasing Power Parity in FX. Real-world frictions are why it can
> temporarily fail.

---

The **Law of One Price (LOOP)** is an economic and financial principle stating that **in an efficient market, identical assets or goods must trade at the exact same price when expressed in a common currency.**

---

## 1. Core Concept & Logic

If two identical assets traded at different prices in the same market, traders would immediately step in to profit without taking on risk:

1. **Buy** the cheaper asset.

2. **Sell** the more expensive asset.

This process is called **arbitrage**. The simultaneous buying pressure on the cheaper asset drives its price **up**, while selling pressure on the expensive asset pushes its price **down**. This rapid trading continues until the prices align and the risk-free profit opportunity disappears.

> **Key Takeaway:** The Law of One Price is the foundational assumption behind the **no-arbitrage principle**, which is used to price derivatives (like options, futures, and swaps).

---

## 2. Key Applications in Finance

### A. Derivatives Valuation (Option Pricing)

As seen in the derivation of **Put-Call Parity** (see [[30 Put-Call Parity]] and [[31 Proof of Put-Call Parity]]):

- A **Portfolio A** (Long Call + Risk-Free Bond) and a **Portfolio B** (Long Put + Underlying Stock) yield the exact same cash flows at maturity across all market conditions.

- By the Law of One Price, because their terminal payoffs are identical, their market prices today _must_ be equal:

$$P_t + S_t = C_t + K e^{-r(T-t)}$$

### B. Foreign Exchange (Purchasing Power Parity - PPP)

In international economics, LOOP suggests that after adjusting for exchange rates, identical goods should cost the same amount in different countries:

$$P_{\text{Domestic}} = E \times P_{\text{Foreign}}$$

_(Where $E$ is the nominal exchange rate)._

---

## 3. Real-World Frictions (Why It Can Fail)

In practice, prices for the same asset or commodity can temporarily or structurally differ due to:

- **Transaction Costs & Fees:** Brokerage commissions, exchange fees, and bid-ask spreads reduce arbitrage margins.

- **Transport Costs & Tariffs:** For physical goods, moving assets across borders adds costs.

- **Market Illiquidity:** If an asset cannot be bought or sold instantly, traders face execution risk.

- **Capital & Regulatory Constraints:** Restrictions on short-selling or capital controls can prevent arbitrageurs from closing the price gap.

## 4. The Thread Running Through the Whole Course

The Law of One Price is the quiet axiom behind almost everything in these notes:

- It is why a **replicating portfolio** must cost the same as the derivative it copies ([[20 Proof of Replicating Portfolio]], [[21 Martingale Pricing Of European Contingent Claims]]).
- It forces the existence of a **risk-neutral measure** — the First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing states *no arbitrage $\iff$ an equivalent martingale measure exists* ([[13 Probability Measures]], [[16 Equivalent Probability Measures and Girsanov's Theorem]]).
- It underpins **put–call parity** ([[30 Put-Call Parity]]) and the cost-of-carry **forward price** ([[22 General Formula]]).

On the theory side it is the mechanism that makes markets **informationally efficient**: prices that fully reflect available information leave no free lunch. That is precisely the claim examined — and qualified — by the [[../Financial Theory/13 - Efficient Market Hypothesis|Efficient Market Hypothesis]] and its [[../Financial Theory/15 - EMH Limitations and Anomalies|anomalies]].
