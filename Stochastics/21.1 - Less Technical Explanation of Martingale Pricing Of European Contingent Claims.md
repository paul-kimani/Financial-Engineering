# 21.1 — Less Technical Explanation of Martingale Pricing of European Contingent Claims

> The same derivation as [[21 - Martingale Pricing Of European Contingent Claims]],
> told with the intuition foregrounded. Start from **no-arbitrage** (a portfolio
> that matches a derivative everywhere must cost the same today), build the
> self-financing replicating portfolio, discount it into a $\mathbb{Q}$-martingale,
> and read off the price as the discounted expected payoff.

---

## 1. The Setup — Why Replicating Portfolios Matter

This page sets up the core philosophy behind why we are allowed to price options in the first place: **The No-Arbitrage Principle**.

- **The Replicating Portfolio Idea:**

    If you have a complex derivative $X$ (like a European call option), you can't easily see its price. But what if you could build a portfolio out of normal things we _can_ price—like a specific number of stocks ($\phi_t$) and risk-free bonds ($\psi_t$)?

    If the value of your portfolio ($V_t$) matches the derivative's value ($V_t^X$) at every single moment up to maturity $T$, then they _must_ cost the exact same amount today. If they didn't, traders would buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one, and make free riskless money (arbitrage).

- **The Martingale Approach:**

    Your notes point out that this entire pricing framework rests on four pillars:

    1. The **Martingale Property** (the asset has no expected trend under the risk-neutral measure).

    2. A **Pre-visible Process** (you choose your stock holdings using only past data, no look-ahead bias).

    3. A **Self-Financing** portfolio (no money enters or leaves the portfolio).

    4. **Replication** (the portfolio perfectly mimics the option at the end).

---

## 2. Starting the Derivation (Steps 1 & 2)

At the top, we see the holy grail formula we want to prove:

$$V_t = E^\mathbb{Q}[\text{Derivative Payoff} \mid \mathcal{F}_t] e^{-r(T-t)}$$

This is the **General Risk-Neutral Valuation Formula**. It says the value of the option today is the average (expectation) of its future payoff under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, discounted back to today.

To prove it, the notes outline the first two steps:

- **Step 1: Setting up the portfolio**

    We want to price a claim with payoff $V_T$ at maturity. We construct a self-financing portfolio $\Pi_t$ of stocks ($S_t$) and bonds ($B_t$):

    $$\Pi_t = \phi_t S_t + \psi_t B_t$$

    For perfect replication, we must have $V_T = \Pi_T$ at the very end.

- **Step 2: Finding the portfolio's SDE**

    Since the portfolio is self-financing, its dynamic change over an infinitesimal second $dt$ is:

    $$d\Pi_t = \phi_t dS_t + \psi_t dB_t$$

    Now we need to see how this evolves under our risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$. Let's turn to the next page to see the math play out.

---

## 3. Calculating the Dynamics and Introducing Itô (Steps 2 & 3)

- **Finishing Step 2:**

    We substitute the risk-neutral dynamics of the stock ($dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q}$) and the bond ($dB_t = r B_t dt$) into our portfolio change equation:

    $$d\Pi_t = \phi_t (r S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q}) + \psi_t (r B_t dt)$$

    If we group the $dt$ terms, we get:

    $$d\Pi_t = r \underbrace{(\phi_t S_t + \psi_t B_t)}_{\Pi_t} dt + \phi_t \sigma S_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q}$$

    $$d\Pi_t = r \Pi_t dt + \phi_t \sigma S_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q}$$

    _The Intuition:_ This SDE tells us that our portfolio grows at the risk-free rate $r$, plus some random volatility driven by our stock exposure.

- **Step 3: Discounting the Portfolio ($\tilde{\Pi}_t = \Pi_t e^{-rt}$)**

    To prove this is a martingale, we must discount it. We apply Itô's Lemma to the function $f(\Pi_t, t) = \Pi_t e^{-rt}$.

    Taking the partial derivatives:

    - $\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = -r \Pi_t e^{-rt}$

    - $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \Pi_t} = e^{-rt}$

    - $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial \Pi_t^2} = 0$ (since the function is linear in $\Pi_t$)

    Plugging these into the Itô formula, we get:

    $$d\tilde{\Pi}_t = e^{-rt} d\Pi_t - r \Pi_t e^{-rt} dt$$

---

## 4. The Martingale Step (Steps 3, 4 & 5)

- **Finishing Step 3:**

    We plug our $d\Pi_t$ expression from the previous page into our newly minted $d\tilde{\Pi}_t$ formula:

    $$d\tilde{\Pi}_t = e^{-rt} \left[ r \Pi_t dt + \phi_t \sigma S_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q} \right] - r \Pi_t e^{-rt} dt$$

    When we expand this, the $+r \Pi_t e^{-rt} dt$ and $-r \Pi_t e^{-rt} dt$ terms cancel each other out completely! We are left with:

    $$d\tilde{\Pi}_t = \phi_t e^{-rt} \sigma S_t dW_t^\mathbb{Q}$$

    Because there is no $dt$ term, this is a **pure martingale**.

- **Step 4: Applying the Martingale Property**

    Since the discounted portfolio is a martingale under $\mathbb{Q}$, its value today ($t$) must equal its expected value at maturity ($T$):

    $$\tilde{\Pi}_t = E^\mathbb{Q}[\tilde{\Pi}_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$$

    Let's write out the discounted terms:

    $$\Pi_t e^{-rt} = E^\mathbb{Q}[\Pi_T e^{-rT} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$$

- **Step 5: Perfect Replication and the Final Formula**

    Since our portfolio perfectly replicates the European option, the portfolio value at maturity must equal the option payoff ($\Pi_T = V_T$):

    $$\Pi_t e^{-rt} = E^\mathbb{Q}[V_T e^{-rT} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$$

    To isolate the option price today ($\Pi_t$), we multiply both sides by $e^{rt}$:

    $$\Pi_t = e^{rt} E^\mathbb{Q}[V_T e^{-rT} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$$

    Since $e^{rt}$ and $e^{-rT}$ are deterministic, we can pull them together inside the expectation to get the final result:

    $$\Pi_t = E^\mathbb{Q}[V_T e^{-r(T-t)} \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$$

This proves that the fair price of the option today is simply the discounted risk-neutral expectation of its payoff!
