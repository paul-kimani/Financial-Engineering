### a) How a Bond's Principal Payment Structure Affects Risk and Return

The timing and structure of principal repayments fundamentally alter a bond's exposure to interest rate risk (duration), reinvestment risk, and credit risk.

- **Bullet Bonds (Maturity Paid at Once):**
    
    - **Risk:** Higher **interest rate risk** (higher duration) because the entire principal is back-loaded to the maturity date. This makes the bond's price highly sensitive to interest rate fluctuations.
        
    - **Return:** Typically offers a higher yield to maturity (YTM) compared to an amortizing bond of the same maturity to compensate for the higher duration risk.
        
- **Amortizing Bonds (Scheduled Principal Repayments, e.g., Sinking Funds):**
    
    - **Risk:** Lower interest rate risk due to a shorter average life (lower duration). However, they introduce higher **reinvestment risk**, as the investor must constantly find ways to reinvest the periodic principal payments in potentially lower-rate environments. **Credit risk** declines over time as the issuer pays down the principal outstanding.
        
    - **Return:** Generally offers a lower yield than a bullet bond due to the lower price sensitivity and defensive repayment structure.
        

### b) The Effect of Contingency Provisions on Investor's Risk and Return

Contingency provisions (embedded options) give either the issuer or the investor the right to force an action, drastically shifting the risk-return landscape.

- **Callable Bonds (Issuer Option to Buy Back):**
    
    - **Risk:** High risk for the investor (**reinvestment risk** and **call risk**). Issuers typically call bonds when interest rates fall to refinance cheaper, leaving investors with cash to reinvest at lower prevailing rates. It also exhibits "negative convexity," meaning its price appreciation is capped as rates drop.
        
    - **Return:** To compensate for this disadvantage, callable bonds offer a **higher coupon rate** and a higher yield (Yield-to-Worst) than identical non-callable bonds.
        
- **Putable Bonds (Investor Option to Sell Back):**
    
    - **Risk:** Highly protective for the investor (**lowers risk**). If interest rates rise and the bond price drops, the investor can put the bond back to the issuer at par and reinvest at the new, higher market rates.
        
    - **Return:** Because this option heavily benefits the investor, putable bonds pay a **lower coupon rate**and have a lower yield than non-putable counterparts.
        

### c) Two Examples of Negative Covenants

Negative covenants are legally binding prohibitions within a bond indenture designed to protect senior lenders and bondholders from actions management might take that would jeopardize the issuer's creditworthiness.

1. **Restrictions on Incurring Additional Debt:**
    
    - _Explanation/Example:_ A covenant stating that the firm cannot issue any new senior debt unless its Interest Coverage Ratio (EBIT / Interest Expense) remains above a certain threshold (e.g., $3.5\times$). This prevents management from diluting the claims of existing bondholders and increasing default risk.
        
2. **Restrictions on Asset Disposals and Distributions (Dividends):**
    
    - _Explanation/Example:_ A covenant prohibiting the firm from selling off core operating assets or paying out cash dividends to equity shareholders if its cash reserves drop below a specified minimum level. This ensures that valuable collateral and liquid capital remain within the corporate entity to service the debt obligations first.
        

### d) Credit Risk Exposure: Revenue Bonds vs. General Obligation (GO) Bonds

This distinction is central to municipal and public sector fixed-income instruments.

- **General Obligation (GO) Bonds:**
    
    - **Credit Risk:** **Lower credit risk.** GO bonds are backed by the "full faith, credit, and taxing power" of the issuing government entity. If money is tight, the municipality can legally raise property, income, or sales taxes across the entire local economy to meet its debt obligations.
        
- **Revenue Bonds:**
    
    - **Credit Risk:** **Higher credit risk.** These bonds are serviced _strictly_ by the revenues generated from a specific standalone project (e.g., toll roads, airports, water treatment plants, or public housing). If the project underperforms or experiences a structural decline in demand, the issuer has no legal obligation or mechanism to tap general tax revenues to make bondholders whole.
