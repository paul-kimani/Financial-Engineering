# 09.1 — Expected Utility Theory Question

> A worked application of [[09 - Expected Utility Theory]]: the same ice-cream vs.
> umbrella investment under two utility functions. Under the convex $U(I) = I^2$
> the **risk-seeking** investor picks the volatile ice-cream business; under the
> concave $U(I) = \sqrt{I}$ the **risk-averse** investor flips to the safer
> umbrellas. The decision reversal shows how the curvature of $U$ — its second
> derivative — encodes risk preference.

---

![[Images/Screenshot 2026-06-02 at 09.33.44.png]]

Let's break this down. When making investment decisions under uncertainty, we evaluate choices based on their **Expected Utility (EU)** rather than just their expected monetary value.

The formula for Expected Utility is the sum of the utility of each possible outcome, weighted by its probability:

$$EU = \sum p_i \cdot U(I_i)$$

Given your utility function is $U(I) = I^2$,

---

## Part A — Convex Utility $U(I) = I^2$

### 1. Expected Utility of Ice-cream

- **Sunny Season:** $U(121) = 121^2 = 14,641$
- **Rainy Season:** $U(25) = 25^2 = 625$

Now, apply the probabilities:

$$EU_{\text{ice-cream}} = \left(\frac{1}{3} \cdot 14641\right) + \left(\frac{2}{3} \cdot 625\right)$$

$$EU_{\text{ice-cream}} = 4880.33 + 416.67$$

$$EU_{\text{ice-cream}} = \mathbf{5297}$$

### 2. Expected Utility of Umbrellas

- **Sunny Season:** $U(64) = 64^2 = 4,096$
- **Rainy Season:** $U(49) = 49^2 = 2,401$

Apply the probabilities:

$$EU_{\text{umbrellas}} = \left(\frac{1}{3} \cdot 4096\right) + \left(\frac{2}{3} \cdot 2401\right)$$

$$EU_{\text{umbrellas}} = 1365.33 + 1600.67$$

$$EU_{\text{umbrellas}} = \mathbf{2966}$$

### The Decision

Since $5297 > 2966$, one would invest in **Ice-cream**.

**The Quantitative Insight:**

Notice _why_ ice cream wins here. The umbrella business is much safer (incomes of 64 and 49 are close together), while the ice cream business is highly volatile (121 vs 25).

However, the utility function $U(I) = I^2$ is convex (its second derivative is positive). In market microstructure and utility theory, a convex utility function perfectly models a **risk-seeking** investor. This investor heavily overweights extreme positive payoffs, which is why the massive 121 payout in the sunny season makes the highly volatile ice-cream investment look incredibly attractive to them, completely overshadowing the safer umbrella option.

---

## Part B — Concave Utility $U(I) = \sqrt{I}$

![[Images/Screenshot 2026-06-02 at 09.35.29.png]]

Let's break this down by running the exact same business scenario from the previous slide, but applying this new utility function: $U(I) = \sqrt{I}$.

### 1. Expected Utility of Ice-cream (High Risk)

- **Sunny Season** ($p = \frac{1}{3}$): $U(121) = \sqrt{121} = 11$
- **Rainy Season** ($p = \frac{2}{3}$): $U(25) = \sqrt{25} = 5$

$$EU_{\text{ice-cream}} = \left(\frac{1}{3} \cdot 11\right) + \left(\frac{2}{3} \cdot 5\right) = 3.67 + 3.33 = \mathbf{7.00}$$

### 2. Expected Utility of Umbrellas (Low Risk)

- **Sunny Season** ($p = \frac{1}{3}$): $U(64) = \sqrt{64} = 8$
- **Rainy Season** ($p = \frac{2}{3}$): $U(49) = \sqrt{49} = 7$

$$EU_{\text{umbrellas}} = \left(\frac{1}{3} \cdot 8\right) + \left(\frac{2}{3} \cdot 7\right) = 2.67 + 4.67 = \mathbf{7.33}$$

### The Result

The decision completely flips. Because $7.33 > 7.00$, an investor with this utility function will choose the safer **Umbrella** business.

---

## Answering the Slide's Questions

**1. Which utility function shows risk aversion?**

$U(I) = \sqrt{I}$ is **risk averse**.

Mathematically, this is a _concave_ function (it flattens out as income increases). This models the economic principle of diminishing marginal utility: an extra shilling when you are poor increases your happiness much more than an extra shilling when you are already rich. Because high extreme payouts are mathematically "discounted" by the square root, this investor dislikes volatility and prefers the certainty of the umbrella business.

**2. Which is risk loving?**

$U(I) = I^2$ is **risk loving** (or risk-seeking).

This is a _convex_ function (it gets steeper as income increases). Here, the marginal utility of wealth is increasing. This investor places a disproportionately massive value on the chance to hit the jackpot, making them willing to accept the high volatility of the ice cream business.

**3. Different utility functions give different risk preferences?**

Yes, absolutely. In quantitative finance, an investor's risk profile isn't just a subjective feeling; it is strictly defined by the mathematical shape (the second derivative) of their utility curve.

---

## The Sign of $U''$ Is the Whole Story

The decision reversal is governed entirely by the **second derivative** of the utility function — which is exactly the object the [[12 - Pratt-Arrow Risk Aversion|Pratt–Arrow]] measure formalises:

$$A(w) = -\frac{U''(w)}{U'(w)}$$

- $U(I) = \sqrt{I}$ is concave ($U'' < 0 \Rightarrow A > 0$): **risk averse**, so it discounts the volatile ice-cream jackpot and picks umbrellas.
- $U(I) = I^2$ is convex ($U'' > 0 \Rightarrow A < 0$): **risk seeking**, so it overweights the jackpot and picks ice-cream.

The same excess-return-for-risk that a risk-averse agent demands here is the **risk premium** $\mu - r$ that shows up on the pricing side as the market price of risk $\theta = \tfrac{\mu-r}{\sigma}$ — the quantity Girsanov's change of measure strips out in [[../Stochastics/13 Probability Measures|the risk-neutral world]]. Preferences and pricing are two views of one idea.
