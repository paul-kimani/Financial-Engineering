# 02 — Consumption and Savings Decision

> The simplest concrete problem in the theory of choice: a two-period
> world, an initial wealth $W_0$, and a single decision — how much to
> consume now versus how much to save for the future. Everything in
> Part I builds on this setup.

---

## 1. The two-period world

An individual lives for two periods, *now* (time $0$) and *later*
(time $1$). They begin with current wealth $W_0$ shillings and make
**one decision**:

- $C_0$ — current consumption (spend today).
- $W_0 - C_0$ — the amount **saved** to support future consumption.

If they save and earn nothing, future consumption is $C_1 = W_0 - C_0$.
If they save and invest (real investment or capital-market lending),
$C_1$ can exceed $W_0 - C_0$.

The whole framework asks: given preferences and opportunities,
what is the **optimal split** $(C_0, C_1)$?

---

## 2. The two ingredients of the choice problem

To solve the consumption/savings decision we need two distinct pieces:

1. **The investor's preferences for current vs future consumption.**
   This is the *want-to* — how does the individual personally trade off
   spending today against spending tomorrow? Captured by **indifference
   curves** ([[04 - Indifference Curves, MRS and Utility]]) which in
   turn obey the **axioms of choice** ([[03 - Axioms of Choice under Certainty]]).

2. **The feasible trade-offs between current and future consumption.**
   This is the *can-do* — what does the market or the production
   technology actually allow? Captured by the **opportunity set**
   ([[05 - Opportunity Set and MRT]]) and, when real investment is
   possible, by the **investment frontier**
   ([[06 - Real Investment Decision]]).

The **optimal decision** sits where the highest attainable indifference
curve just touches the opportunity set — graphically a tangency, and
algebraically the condition $\text{MRS}^\ast = \text{MRT}^\ast$ derived
in chapter [[06 - Real Investment Decision]].

---

## 3. Why "two goods"?

In standard microeconomics the consumer chooses between physical goods
(apples vs oranges). Corporate finance and financial economics replace
those goods with **time periods**:

- **Good 1** — current consumption $C_0$.
- **Good 2** — future consumption $C_1$.

The trade-off is no longer "how many apples per orange?" but **"how
many KES tomorrow am I willing to give up to get one KES today?"** —
or its inverse. That subjective trade-off is the **Marginal Rate of
Substitution (MRS)** developed in chapter
[[04 - Indifference Curves, MRS and Utility]].

The complementary objective trade-off — how much future consumption the
market or technology actually delivers per shilling deferred — is the
**Marginal Rate of Transformation (MRT)** developed in chapter
[[05 - Opportunity Set and MRT]].
